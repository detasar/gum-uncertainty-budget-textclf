# Literature review: Petri & Mari (2026) and the aleatoric/epistemic debate

Prepared 2026-10-17. Every source below was opened or found via search on that date. Where only an abstract or summary was seen, that is stated. Nothing here is a verbatim quote unless it's in quotation marks and attributed to text that was read.

## 1. The paper: what it actually claims

**Dario Petri & Luca Mari, "Uncertainty Evaluation: Bridging Machine Learning and Measurement Science", arXiv:2610.18066 (v1, 13 Oct 2026), cs.LG.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18066
Read in full from the arXiv PDF: 7 pages, 26 references. It has been submitted to IEEE and is not yet peer-reviewed.

- **Type of paper.** Conceptual/position paper. It contains **no experiments, no datasets and no code**. The arXiv page, Hugging Face Papers (404), GitHub repo search (title, arXiv id, authors) and GitHub code search (only aggregator/news repos mirror the abstract) all came up empty. So there is **no official code**, and our experiment is an independent illustration, not a reproduction.
- **Process vs product quality (Sec. II).** Quality measured on a process (calibration, validation or test conditions) gives "an upper bound" on, and is not the same as, the quality of an individual product. They state this applies to both measurements and ML predictions.
- **Metrology's history (Sec. IV).** The pre-GUM random/systematic error split was dropped because (i) the true value is unknowable, and (ii) the split is not inherent to a source; it "may depend on the model adopted". Examples are an accelerometer's temperature effect (random until it is modelled, then systematic) and a quantizer (deterministic, but usefully treated as random). They quote the GUM: "categorizing the methods of evaluating uncertainty components rather than the components themselves avoids [the mentioned] ambiguity". Type A and Type B classify *evaluation methods*, not kinds of uncertainty.
- **ML analogy (Sec. V).** The aleatoric/epistemic split (they cite Hüllermeier & Waegeman 2021) is "analogous, to some extent" to random/systematic. Their criticisms:
  1. It depends on the model, the training procedure and the information used.
  2. There are no agreed definitions, so many non-comparable indices exist (citing Bickford Smith et al., ICML 2025).
  3. It may not capture reducible versus irreducible parts.
  4. Calling aleatoric "irreducible" may encourage premature acceptance of uncertainty levels.
  
  They explicitly say ML is *not* lacking methods (Bayesian, ensembles, conformal, calibration, OOD). The open issue is integrating them into "a more general, process-aware but result-centered framework".
- **Mismatched conditions (Sec. VI).** Distribution shift is mapped to uncompensated systematic effects (the model is still valid but less accurate). OOD inputs are mapped to gross errors (the model is invalid, so the uncertainty evaluation is "meaningless"). They propose two steps: (1) model-scope verification (OOD or gross-error detection), then (2) output plus uncertainty assessment.
- **Conclusion (Sec. VIII).** They argue for moving "beyond the binary classification of aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty toward a GUM-inspired framework, grounded on the systematic identification, quantification and propagation of all recognized uncertainty contributions". They mention definitional and target uncertainty as possible decision boundaries. The wording is hedged throughout: "could provide", "potentially rigorous".
- **What the paper does *not* do.** It gives no worked uncertainty budget for any ML model. It does not specify how to propagate contributions through a learned model, how to handle correlated components, or what plays the role of the measurement model. It does not engage with the defenders of the decomposition (Sec. 3 below).

| Source | Position | Link |
|---|---|---|

| Sale et al., *Label-wise aleatoric and epistemic UQ*, UAI 2024 | Keeps the split and proposes variance-based, label-wise measures that fix some of the entropy problems. | Pecher, Spiegel, Belanec, Cegin, *Revisiting Prompt Sensitivity in LLMs for Text Classification: The Role of Prompt Underspecification*, arXiv:2602.04297 (Feb 2026) | **Contrary evidence for the "wording is structural" reading.** A significant part of observed prompt sensitivity comes from *underspecified* prompts. **Implication.** Importing "the GUM" into ML imports an unresolved foundational debate along with it. Petri & Mari present the GUM as "standardized and internally consistent". On the Bayesian/frequentist point, metrologists themselves (Kacker & Jones; Bich et al.; Elster) dispute that.

## 4. Data scarcity vs structural lack (the philosophical layer)

- **Epistemic, in the reducible-by-data sense.** This uncertainty shrinks as n grows: calibration-set sampling, training-set sampling, test-set sampling.
- **Structural / definitional.** This uncertainty comes from how the task is *specified*: which label set is used, how the classes are worded, what question is asked. More data from the same specification does not remove it. Metrology already has a name for the floor this sets: **definitional uncertainty** (VIM 2.27). Changing the definition (the wording) gives "another definitional uncertainty".
- **Related notions.** Der Kiureghian & Ditlevsen describe the split as a modelling choice. Kirchhof et al. describe *underspecification uncertainty* in LLM agents. Lane & Maxfield (J. Evol. Econ. 15, 2005, https://doi.org/10.1007/s00191-004-0227-7) distinguish truth, semantic and **ontological** uncertainty. The last means uncertainty about which entities and categories exist at all. They developed this for innovation processes, not ML, so the analogy is ours.
- **Knightian uncertainty.** Knight's (1921) risk vs uncertainty distinction is often invoked here. *We did not open a source for it today, so it is mentioned only as background.*
- **What would settle it.** (a) Wording variance stays flat as calibration n grows, while sampling variance falls roughly as 1/√n. Our experiment tests this cheaply on one dataset. (b) Wording variance persists across models and datasets. (c) Wording variance disappears when the label ontology is made precise (e.g., definitions or examples per class), which would indicate an underspecified definition rather than missing data. We only test (a).

Kept out on purpose: the scout brief's other items (LLM-judge reliability, SCUR, the category-theory papers). They are relevant background but not needed for the claims made here.

## 5. What is still open

1. What plays the role of the "measurement model" through which ML contributions propagate? For conformal set size we used a black-box Monte Carlo (S1-style), not a Taylor law of propagation.
2. Contributions are not independent. Wording × model × shift interact, and the RSS assumes they don't.
3. Many ML "components" are discrete choices (model, prompt), not random variables. Treating them as Type B uniform over alternatives is a convention, and the result depends on which alternatives you include.
4. Per-prediction budgets (product-level quality, which is the paper's actual target) vs per-pipeline budgets (what we measure). We only did the latter.
5. GUM's own Bayesian/frequentist inconsistency (Sec. 3) carries over.

## 6. Honest takeaways usable on X

1. Petri & Mari's core point is old and well supported. Der Kiureghian & Ditlevsen (2009) already wrote that the aleatoric/epistemic split "is determined by our modeling choices", and recent ML papers (Bickford Smith et al. 2025, Kirchhof et al. 2025, Mucsányi et al. 2024) find the split incoherent or hard to disentangle in practice.
2. The defenders have real wins. BALD-style epistemic scores do help active learning (Gal et al. 2017), and Kendall & Gal's aleatoric modelling helps robustness. "Model-dependent" does not mean "useless".
3. The GUM is not a finished solution to import. Metrologists still debate its Bayesian/frequentist consistency (Kacker & Jones 2003; Elster 2014; Bich et al. 2012).
4. What the GUM does offer cheaply is the *habit*: list your sources, evaluate each with a stated method (Type A or B), combine them with stated assumptions, and see which one dominates. The paper proposes this but doesn't demonstrate it, and our small experiment is one attempt.
